PART C · RUN IT

Scale, Sustain or Stop

“Staying inside the budget is not success. The question at review is whether the work earned more spend, the same spend or none.”

Review closes the loop. It compares what happened against what the plan said would happen, makes one of three decisions per task class, and hands the result back to the next plan.

Under budget is not a result

Most reviews of AI spend ask one question: did we stay within the allowance? A yes is treated as success. But a task class can come in under budget because it ran less than expected, because it was routed to a cheaper model that failed more often, or because people stopped using its output. All three look the same on a spend report. None of them is good news.

The reverse also holds. A task class that went over budget while its yield per dollar held or improved may be the best candidate for more investment. The budget is a constraint, the fence from the Reframe page. Review is about what grew inside it.

Three decisions, each with its evidence

For each task class, Review ends in one of three decisions. Each needs different evidence, and the evidence should be the measures the plan committed to, not new ones chosen after the results are in.

  • Scale. Yield per dollar at or above the planned level, holding steady across several periods, with completion rates not falling as volume grows and no single expensive run carrying the average. Scaling multiplies whatever the task class does, so the evidence needs to show it is doing the right thing consistently.
  • Sustain. Yield above the floor but below plan, or above plan on too little data to trust. Keep running, keep the attribution in place, and name the next lever to try. Sustain is a decision to keep learning, with a date for the next review.
  • Stop. Yield below the floor for the period the plan stated, after the agreed number of attempts to improve it. Or the work finishes and is kept, but the value hypothesis did not hold: nobody downstream used it. Stopping needs the least evidence to justify and is the hardest to carry out.

Separate the two risks before deciding

A task class that missed its plan needs one more question before any decision: did it miss on price or on yield? The diagnostic rule from the Manage page applies over a review period just as it does to a single spike. If cost rose while yield held, the work is converting and the price assumption broke. That is a case for the routing, caching and rate levers, and rarely a reason to stop. If cost rose and yield fell, the work itself is not converting, and a cheaper model will not change that.

Merging the two leads to the worst outcome in either direction: stopping a task class that worked because its rates went up, or scaling one that does not work because a discount made it look cheap.

WHY THE STOP CONDITION WAS WRITTEN AT PLAN

By the time a task class reaches review it has a team, a workflow and people who rely on it. Arguing about what counts as failure at that point is slow and uncomfortable. The plan settled the floor and the time allowed when nobody had anything to defend. Review only has to check the result against it.

Stop is a finding

A stopped task class is not wasted spend if it was stopped on evidence. It establishes that a certain kind of work, done a certain way, did not convert at an acceptable rate. That is information the next plan can use, and it frees budget for the classes that did convert. Organisations that never stop anything end up spreading the same budget across more and more work with less and less evidence about any of it.

Back to plan

The output of Review is the input to the next Plan: updated baseline cards, floors adjusted by what was observed rather than what was guessed, task classes added or removed, and the next lever named for each class that is being sustained. Each turn of the loop replaces a guess with a measurement. That is the slow route to the numbers the Open Problem page says nobody has yet.

Claim status. This loop is specified, not yet tested at scale. The decisions follow from the measures defined earlier on this hub, and the thresholds that would separate scale from sustain from stop for a given task class are still to be measured. Running the loop is how they would be found.

GO DEEPER: tail concentration and the scale decision

Agentic workloads tend to be tail-heavy: a few runs that loop far longer than the rest can carry a large share of total spend. An average yield per dollar that looks healthy can hide a task class whose median run converts well and whose worst runs burn most of the budget. Before scaling, look at the distribution, not just the mean. If the tail is driving cost, a stop rule or a cost detector on the loop is often a better next step than more volume, because scaling multiplies the tail along with everything else.
