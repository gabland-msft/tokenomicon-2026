A Budget Is Not a Plan

“Write down what the work will cost, what it is for, and the yield you will accept, before the first token is spent.”

The operating cycle applies the measures from the first six pages through Plan, Manage, Optimize and Review, before returning the results to the next plan.

The operating loop: plan, manage, optimize and review

Most AI spend starts with a number, such as a monthly cap, a pilot allowance or a line in someone’s budget, which sets how much may be spent but records nothing about what the spend is supposed to buy or how anyone would know whether it bought it. Months later the team can report that it stayed inside the cap, without anyone being able to say whether that was a good result.

The four pages in this part use the following operating structure: Plan sets expectations before spend, Manage attributes spend as it happens, Optimize changes one thing at a time and checks whether yield moved, and Review decides whether to scale, sustain or stop and feeds the next plan. Similar stage names are common in cost-management practice, and here each is defined by what it decides. Token yield, the measure defined on the earlier pages, is tracked at all four stations.

What a plan has to contain

A plan for agentic work needs three things written down, of which the budget is one.

  1. A cost envelope. The expected spend per unit of work and the assumptions behind it, namely which models are used, what token mix they consume and how much of the input is expected to be cached.
  2. A value hypothesis. What the work is for, stated as something a person outside the team could check. By that test “faster delivery” is a wish, whereas “fewer backlog items waiting more than a week for a first draft” is a hypothesis.
  3. An accepted yield. The share of spend you expect to convert into outcomes, and the floor below which you would change course.

The third item is the one most often missing, and the rest of the operating loop depends on it: without it, Review has nothing to compare results against, and any outcome can be judged acceptable after the fact.

Expected yield as three questions

The yield measures on the Agent Loop page translate into three questions a planner can answer before any data exists: whether the work finished without a human taking over (ACR), whether the output was kept rather than discarded or reworked (TER), and whether the kept output mattered to someone downstream (VGR). The Agent Efficiency Score is the product of the three, and a plan records an expected value and a floor for each, with one named person accountable for the whole.

ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT A TARGET

The numbers on a baseline card are guesses, written down so that they can be checked, because nobody yet knows what a normal yield looks like for a given class of agentic work. Recording the estimate provides a baseline against which observed results can later be compared.

Separate assumptions for price and yield

The Reframe page separated two risks that are easily merged: price risk, which concerns what each token costs (rates, the mix of token classes and cache behaviour), and yield risk, which concerns whether the tokens produced anything worth keeping. A plan should carry a separate assumption for each, because the two fail for different reasons and are fixed by different people. A cached-input share that comes in below the share assumed in the cost envelope is a broken price assumption, and work that finishes but is never used is a broken yield assumption; a single cost-per-outcome forecast that merges the two leaves no way to tell afterwards which one failed.

Planning per task class

A plan can fail without any visible warning when its unit is too coarse, because a single yield figure for “all of our agent use” averages a task class that works well with one that burns money and so hides both. The unit of planning should therefore be the task class, meaning a recognisable kind of work that runs often enough to produce a pattern, such as drafting tests for a changed module or triaging incoming tickets. Each class gets its own card, floor and owner, and the owner is a single named person, so that when a signal fires on the Manage page it is someone’s job to look.

Setting the stop condition in the plan

The hardest decision in the operating loop is made at Review, when a task class has been running for some time and has people attached to it, and the plan is the cheapest place to make that decision easier. The plan should state in advance the result that would cause you to stop, expressed as a yield floor held over a stated period after a stated number of attempts to improve it. Setting the rule before the task class has an operating history reduces the scope for later decisions to be shaped by existing commitments.

GO DEEPER: where the shadow price μ fits in a plan

The budget in a plan is the constraint against which the shadow price μ is defined. Early in a piece of work, with most of the budget unspent, μ is designed to sit low and most upgrades pass the Value of Token test; as spend burns down faster than value arrives, μ is designed to rise and the test tightens. A plan that records both the budget and the expected rate of value delivery therefore supplies the inputs from which μ is computed. Specified, not yet validated: this is how the instrument is meant to behave, not a result observed on production workloads.
