COST VS. YIELD

The Reframe

“Cost isn’t the thing you’re optimizing. It’s a fence. The real question is what your tokens yielded — and that’s structurally harder to observe.”

Cost, quality, and time are constraints on the work, not the objective of it. Confusing the two produces a specific, expensive mistake: cutting price while yield keeps leaking, and calling it optimization.

Cost is a fence, not the goal

The correct objective is to maximize the value a customer actually operates. Cost, quality, and time are constraints on that pursuit: fences around the field, not the crop growing inside it. Treating a constraint as the objective is like minimizing the height of the fence instead of growing what’s inside it: the fence gets shorter, and nobody notices the field went fallow.

That confusion is easy to fall into because the two risks it conflates don’t behave the same way. Price risk (what did each token cost?) is metered today, though not simply: the five-class, multi-surface billing structure on the previous page means “observable” still takes real work to reconcile. Yield risk (did those tokens produce the outcome? did the code ship, get adopted, work correctly?) is harder still: structurally deferred, and partially inaccessible at the moment spend happens. FinOps’s own Quantify Business Value capability already reaches toward this at the workload level. What’s genuinely new here is resolving it at the grain an agentic loop actually operates at (per task class, per loop iteration, before the outcome is known), which is where TokenOps picks up the thread FinOps already started.

Why cheaper tokens don’t fix a yield problem

Cutting price does not fix yield. A cheaper model can save 40% on tokens and still produce output that fails review and gets reworked: net cost goes up, not down, because the saved tokens were never the expensive part. The expensive part was always the spend that didn’t convert.

That’s what token yield names directly: the conversion rate of spend into accepted, operated outcomes. A model that’s 40% cheaper per token and converts at half the rate has made the fence shorter and the field smaller at the same time.

TIMING, NOT MEASUREMENT, IS THE PROBLEM

Spend is committed before yield is known, like a commodity bought before the harvest. That’s structural, not a dashboard gap: no amount of better instrumentation after the fact changes the fact that the money went out the door first. The only lever available at spend time is a leading indicator good enough to act on before the outcome resolves.

A candidate leading indicator: the token mix

Cost per outcome is only available after the fact. The mix of token classes — cached versus fresh input, tool-result input versus output, utilization against provisioned capacity — is available at the current turn, before the outcome is known. My own working research proposes reading specific mix signatures as candidate diagnostics of yield risk, on exactly that basis: none of them requires an outcome label to compute, which is what makes them viable as leading indicators rather than post-hoc analysis.

Observed signature Proposed reading
High cached-token share Stable prefix; loop working as designed
Falling cached tokens, rising fresh input Prefix churn: config changes, rotating context, mutating tool lists
Tool-result input growing faster than output The agent is searching, not building
Output rising with no state change in the environment Grinding
PTU utilization near ceiling with rising 429s Capacity-bound, not cost-bound: a different intervention entirely

Claim status. These signatures are proposed diagnostics. They have not been characterised against outcome data, and no thresholds are asserted here. They’re offered as candidates for specification, not as a measurement already in production. That's the same honesty I try to apply to my own formalism elsewhere.

GO DEEPER — the shadow price μ and token yield’s formal definition

Token yield’s public framing stops at one symbol: μ. My own underlying optimization framework formalizes the rest as a constrained optimization over spend allocation. Specified, not validated: the model below describes the framework as designed, not a result confirmed against production data.

Shadow price μ. μ is the dual variable (Lagrange multiplier) on the budget constraint — it prices the marginal value of one more unit of spend at the current allocation, and is the one symbol carried into the simplified public framing. Value of a Token (VoT), VoT = ΔΠᵢ / Δcᵢ, is the ratio of incremental engagement value to incremental cost when upgrading an item’s configuration; an upgrade is accepted only if VoT clears the hurdle hᵢ = (1 + μ) × Δcᵢ, which tightens automatically as μ rises.

Conversion framing. TER, VGR, ACR, and AES (defined on the Agentic Loop page) sit downstream of this: they measure whether spend converted to accepted, value-generating, autonomously-completed outcomes. That's the same spend-to-yield conversion μ is pricing at the margin.
